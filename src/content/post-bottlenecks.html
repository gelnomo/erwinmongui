Delivery

Finding delivery bottlenecks with lead time and cycle time.

When work slows down, the cause is rarely where people first point. I use lead time, cycle time and a few flow metrics to find exactly where stories get stuck, check the pattern over several sprints, and confirm the root cause with the engineers before changing anything. AI tools make the analysis faster, and they also create a new bottleneck to watch.

By Erwin MonguiSeptember 25, 20266 min read
In short

Lead time is how long a request takes from the moment it is asked for until it is delivered. Cycle time is how long it takes once someone starts working on it. A big gap between them means work is waiting, not being worked on. Measure both per stage, confirm the pattern with the team, change one thing, and measure again.

Four numbers that describe flow

MetricWhat it measures
Lead timeFrom request to delivery, including all the waiting
Cycle timeFrom starting work to delivery
Work in progress (WIP)How many items are open at the same time
ThroughputHow many items are finished per week or sprint

They are connected. Little's Law says that, on average, cycle time equals work in progress divided by throughput. In practice: if a team starts more work than it finishes, everything takes longer, even if everyone is busy. Reducing work in progress is often the fastest way to speed up delivery.

A fifth number, flow efficiency, is the share of cycle time when someone is actively working on an item. On many teams it is surprisingly low, because most of the time is spent waiting for review, testing, a decision or another team.

The five steps

  1. Measure both times for every story. Break cycle time into the stages of your workflow, such as in progress, in review, in testing and waiting to deploy.
  2. Find the deviation. Look for the stage where time grows most compared with normal.
  3. Check it is a pattern. Compare across the sprint or release. One slow story is noise; the same stage slowing down every sprint is a bottleneck.
  4. Talk to the engineers involved. The data shows where; people explain why. Common causes are dependencies on other teams, unclear requirements and slow code reviews.
  5. Change one thing and measure again. Adjust priorities, the workflow or the review process, then check whether the numbers move.

Common causes and fixes

Where time growsLikely causeWhat usually helps
Before work startsUnclear requirementsRefine stories with product before the sprint, with clear acceptance criteria
In progressWaiting on another teamSurface dependencies in planning, agree on API contracts early
In progressToo much work in progressSet WIP limits and finish work before starting more
In reviewReviews pile upSmaller pull requests, agreed review times, AI-assisted first-pass review
In testingManual testingAutomated tests in CI, with AI assistants helping write them

Where AI helps, and the new bottleneck it creates

AI tools help at both ends of this process:

  • Analysis. Export your ticket history from Jira or your pull request data from GitHub, and an AI assistant can compute time per stage, spot outliers and suggest patterns in minutes. Treat its findings as hypotheses to check, not conclusions.
  • Fixes. AI coding assistants help write the missing tests, and AI review tools can do a first pass on pull requests, so human reviewers focus on design and risk.

But when a team adopts AI coding assistants, writing code gets faster, and more of it arrives at review and testing at once. The bottleneck moves downstream. If cycle time doesn't improve after adopting AI, look at the review and testing stages first. The fix is usually smaller pull requests, clear review expectations and better automated testing, not more code generation.

Why validate with people

Numbers without context lead to the wrong fix. A long review stage might mean reviewers are overloaded, or that pull requests are too big to review, and each needs a different change. Asking first keeps the fix targeted and keeps the team's trust.
